How tech hiring actually works

Most tech roles move through the same five or six stages. Each one checks something different, so prepare for the stage you're in. The practice tabs map to these stages.

1

Application & resume screen

Checks: keywords and a clear story. Many companies use applicant tracking systems that scan for words from the job post.

Prep: mirror the post's language on your resume, put your best project (Ask BPL) near the top with a link, and keep LinkedIn and GitHub current. A referral from a real person beats a cold application by a wide margin, so ask your Pursuit network.

2

Recruiter pre-screen · 20–30 min, phone or video

Checks: communication, basic fit, motivation, salary range, timeline, work authorization and location.

Prep: a 60-second "tell me about yourself," a researched "why this company," your salary range, and your start date. Practice with the Mock pre-screen tab.

3

Hiring manager interview · 30–60 min

Checks: whether you can do this specific job and work well with this team. Mostly behavioral and situational questions.

Prep: five or six STAR stories that cover customers, conflict, failure, leadership, learning fast and teaching. Practice in Question cards → Behavioral.

4

Technical or skills assessment

Checks: the hands-on skills in the post. This can be a timed online test (HackerRank, CodeSignal), a take-home project, a live exercise, a mock customer call, a product demo or a training sample.

Prep: for engineering, timed easy problems three times a week (Code warm-ups). For customer success, solutions or enablement roles, practice explaining a technical concept to a non-technical person and running a 10-minute demo of your own project.

5

Final round or panel · 2–4 hours

Checks: consistency across several interviewers, plus "would I want to work with this person every day?"

Prep: different stories for different interviewers, since they compare notes. Bring two or three questions for each person. Eat beforehand and keep water nearby.

6

Offer & negotiation

Checks: nothing. You've already won the job, and now it's a conversation.

Prep: it's normal and expected to ask for time and to negotiate politely. See the negotiation notes below.

Before every interview

  • Read the job post twice and pick the 3 skills they care about most
  • Study the company site, recent news and their product. Try the product if you can
  • Look up your interviewer on LinkedIn
  • Match one of your stories to each of those 3 skills
  • Write down 3 questions to ask them
  • Find the pay range (NYC posts must list one) and set your number
  • Video calls: test camera, mic and lighting. Plain background, laptop at eye level
  • Keep your resume and a few notes open, but don't read from them

During the interview

  • Answer in 1–2 minutes, then stop. Offer more: "Happy to go deeper on that"
  • Say "I" for what you did and "we" for the team result
  • Use real numbers where you can: 4 venues, 9 years, team of 4
  • It's fine to pause: "Good question, let me think for a second"
  • If you don't know a technical answer, say so and explain how you'd find out
  • Never criticize a former employer or teammate
  • Close by repeating why you're excited about this specific role

Common mistakes that cost offers

Rambling. Long answers lose the listener. Use STAR and time yourself.

Generic "why us." If the answer fits any company, it fits none. Name something specific.

Underselling a career change. Hospitality, teaching and performing are real experience in client management, training and composure under pressure. Translate them; don't apologize for them.

No questions at the end. It can read as low interest. Always have three ready.

Bluffing technical answers. Interviewers can tell. "I haven't used that yet, but here's how I'd learn it" scores better.

No follow-up. Send a thank-you within 24 hours. Few candidates do, so it stands out.

Negotiating an offer

  • Thank them and say you're excited, then ask for a few days to review it
  • Ask for the full offer in writing: base pay, bonus, equity, benefits, PTO and start date
  • Compare with the posted range and market data before responding
  • Ask once, politely, with a reason: "Based on the range and my experience in X, would you consider [number]?"
  • If base pay is fixed, ask about a sign-on bonus, start date, learning budget or title
  • Get any final agreement in writing before you resign or accept

Handling the career-change question

Expect some version of "You're new to tech, so why should we hire you?" Answer it confidently in three beats:

  1. Own it: "I'm newer to tech, but not new to work."
  2. Translate it: "I've trained staff, managed four venues and handled thousands of customers. That's client management and enablement."
  3. Prove it: "And I've shipped real AI tools, like Ask BPL for Brooklyn Public Library."
